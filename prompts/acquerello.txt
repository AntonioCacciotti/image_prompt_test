OUTPUT FORMAT — DECIDE THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE
The output is ALWAYS a VERTICAL image with a 3:4 aspect ratio, taller than wide,
whatever the shape of the source photograph. If the photo is horizontal or
square, recompose the same subjects into a vertical 3:4 sheet by extending the
WHITE PAPER above and below them — never by extending the bodies. Never crop or
cut the subjects to fit.
Transform this portrait photo into a premium editorial watercolor illustration
on pure white paper: a hand-painted gallery portrait, fully resolved at the
faces and dissolving into free pigment at its edges.
## SUBJECT FIDELITY — ABSOLUTELY CRITICAL
Output EXACTLY the same number of subjects as the source photo, same poses, same
relative positions. Never add people, children, partners or pets.
Each person must stay immediately recognizable to someone who knows them:
preserve head and face shape, eye shape and spacing, eyebrow shape, nose
proportions, mouth width, cheekbones, jawline, chin, hairline, hairstyle, hair
colour and length, glasses, facial hair, real skin tone, apparent age, weight,
ethnicity and expression. Different subjects must clearly read as different
individuals. Never harden, age, coarsen, thin or thicken a face; never make
anyone look worse than in the photo. That structure is RENDERED IN PAINT, not
copied: simplification is expected, distortion of the person is not.
## WHAT SURVIVES FROM THE PHOTOGRAPH — ONE RULE
Apply this test to every single thing in the source photo, one at a time:
  KEEP it if a subject touches it — holds it, wears it, leans on it, sits on it,
  or stands directly behind it.
  DELETE it if nobody touches it, whatever it is.
Nothing else survives: no place, no setting, no distance, no other people.
SURFACES GO: floors, ground, tables, counters, walls. Kept objects rest on bare
white paper, not on anything. The one exception is a seat a person is actually
sitting on, which stays because the pose needs it.
Kept objects are painted in the same position and the same size as in the photo,
fully readable — lettering and logos included — in the same watercolor treatment
as the figures. Never delete, shrink or move them.
WHEN IN DOUBT whether something belongs to the people or to the place, KEEP IT.
Losing a bottle, a pet, a bouquet or a cake that mattered to them is far worse
than leaving one extra object on the paper.
COLOUR: never carry a colour from the deleted surroundings into the painting.
Green pigment only if a subject actually wears green.
## FRAMING FIDELITY — ABSOLUTELY CRITICAL
Paint exactly what the photograph shows of each body: all of it, and nothing
more.
WHAT THE PHOTO SHOWS IS PAINTED IN FULL: if legs, feet or hands are visible,
they are all there and anatomically complete. Never crop, cut, amputate or
truncate a limb the photograph shows.
WHAT THE PHOTO DOES NOT SHOW DOES NOT EXIST: never invent, complete, extend or
imagine any part of a body the photograph does not contain. If the photo is a
head-and-shoulders crop, the painting is a head-and-shoulders crop. If it stops
at the waist, the painting stops at the waist. NEVER add legs, feet, hips,
hands, arms or a lower body that the photograph does not show, and never invent
a chair, a seat or a surface to support an invented pose.
WHERE THE PHOTOGRAPH CUTS A BODY, THE PAINT DISSOLVES: the washes loosen, break
and fade into the white paper exactly at that point, so the figure ends in
fading pigment rather than in a straight horizontal edge. Never end a figure
with a hard cut line.
## GRADIENT OF FINISH
One continuous watercolor painting whose finish loosens from the faces downward.
The zones are measured on the FIGURES' OWN HEIGHT, not on the image frame.
ZONE 1 — HEADS AND FACES: clean flat washes with controlled modelling. Smooth
and liquid, clearly defined, never scratchy — and never photographic.
THE FACE MUST READ AS PAINT, NOT AS A PHOTOGRAPH — CRITICAL
Three signals must all be present on every face:
  1. PAPER HIGHLIGHTS — the brightest planes (forehead, bridge and tip of the
     nose, top of the cheekbone, chin) are BARE UNPAINTED PAPER with visible
     irregular edges, not smoothly lit skin.
  2. VISIBLE WASH BOUNDARIES — in two or three places on each face, a clear edge
     where one wash stopped and the next began: along the jaw, at the temple, at
     the hairline, where the face meets the neck.
  3. SIMPLIFIED LIGHT — many subtle values in the photograph become three or
     four decided ones. No continuous photographic gradient across a face, no
     airbrushed skin, no rendered pores.
VALUES: three or four steps on the skin, no more — bare white paper for the
lights, one clean even wash of the true skin tone for the mid, one or two shapes
of a deeper tone for the shadows.
LOST AND FOUND EDGES: shadow edges are CRISP where they follow real structure
(jawline, side of the nose, hairline, eye socket) and SOFT where they cross the
skin itself (a cheek, the forehead). At least half the shadow edges must be
crisp and visible as paint.
FEATURES ARE DRAWN, NOT MODELLED: eyes, eyelids, eyebrows, nostrils, the line
between the lips and the corners of the mouth are stated with a few small, dark,
decisive marks, crisp as a fine brush placed once. Eyes stay bright and clean,
with white paper as their highlight. Faces read sharp from a distance and stay
fully recognizable even when the subjects are small in the frame.
HAIR: two or three large FLAT masses of value with clean, sharp, slightly ragged
edges — light, mid, dark — smooth inside, no texture, no dry brush. On top of
them no more than eight to twelve individual strokes in total for an entire head
of hair. Never individual strands, never fine hatching.
ZONE 2 — TORSOS, ARMS AND CLOTHING: progressively looser. Edges soften and
break. Garments become confident single-stroke washes with visible brush
direction. Some contours are left open, letting white paper into the figure.
ZONE 3 — THE LAST 10-15% OF THE FIGURES' HEIGHT: silhouette edges break into
wet-on-wet blooms, granulation and backruns. The dissolution happens at the CONTOUR
of the body, not across it: anatomy stays readable and complete. Shoes and feet
stay recognizable.
## LINEWORK
A few fine dark lines in a warm brown-black, only where the drawing needs
structure: the contour of a hand and the separations between fingers, the edge
of a jaw against a shadow, the crease of an eyelid, where a sleeve meets an arm.
Thin, tapering and BROKEN — each appears for a short stretch and disappears.
Never a continuous outline around a figure, never an ink-drawn or comic look.
Fewer lines than feels necessary.
## THE SURROUNDING PAINT — ONE FAINT CONTINUOUS WASH
The figures sit inside a soft halo of watercolour. It is DENSEST where it meets
their bodies — against a shoulder, along a flank, behind an arm, in the gap
between two people — and FADES OUTWARD until it disappears, so the paint has no
outer boundary, only a dissolution into white paper.
VERY DILUTE — THE STRENGTH OF THE WASH, AND THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE HERE: this
is a faint tint, not a field of colour. At its strongest point it is NO DARKER
than the palest shade of skin in the picture, and over most of its area it is
barely more than a breath of colour on white paper. The portrait must read as
people on a white sheet with a quiet trace of colour around them — never as
people standing in front of a coloured background.
BUILD IT AS ONE CONTINUOUS WASH, NOT AS SEPARATE MARKS: a single unbroken mass
of colour surrounding the group, solid where it touches the bodies and breaking
up only at its outer edge. Its overall shape is irregular, its edges are wet and
blooming, and here and there a visible boundary shows where one pour of colour
met another — the mark of paint that was laid, not sprayed. It is never a smooth
airbrushed gradient and never a field of separate spots.
A light scatter of spatter dots at the outer fringes. THE LOWER EDGE BEHAVES
EXACTLY LIKE THE SIDES: it frays, blooms and fades into the white paper in the
same way, with no drips, no runs and no pigment travelling down toward the
bottom of the sheet.
IT BEHAVES LIKE REAL WATERCOLOUR AT ITS OUTER EDGE: the outer rim bleeds and
melts into the paper, its colour thinning into the white rather than stopping at
a defined line, so the halo dissolves the way a wet wash dries on damp paper.
The halo surrounds the group on both sides and below, rising behind the
shoulders to about ear height. It is irregular: wider on one side than on the
other, thick in one place and absent a hand's width away.
COLOURS — SOFT AND WARM, TAKEN FROM THE PEOPLE: the wash uses only TWO tints,
plus the bare paper. Take the dominant WARM tone and the dominant COOL tone from
the subjects themselves — their skin, hair and clothing — and soften them well
below the saturation they have in the photograph, keeping A CLEAR TRACE of the
original hue.
THE TINT IS UNMISTAKABLY A COLOUR, ONLY A VERY LIGHT ONE — not a grey with a
hint of temperature. A viewer should see a pale warm colour, not a warm-ish
grey. Keep it light and keep it soft, but keep it a colour.
THE WARM TINT DOMINATES: about three quarters of the wash is the warm one — a
soft sunlit greige, like light on paper — and the cool grey appears only in the
minority, as relief. Never a flat cold grey, never the two in equal measure: the
halo should feel like warm light, not like dust.
No fully saturated colour anywhere in it: nothing that reads as a bright pink,
yellow or blue. The halo accompanies the portrait; it never introduces a colour
that is not already quietly present in the people.
IT NEVER COMPETES WITH THE PEOPLE — THE RULE THAT OUTRANKS THE REST OF THIS
BLOCK: the faces are what the eye goes to first, always. If any part of the wash
is strong enough to be noticed before the people, it is too dark or too
saturated.
THE WASH GOES AROUND OBJECTS, NEVER OVER THEM: where a bottle, a bucket or any
other object stands, the paint stops at its edge and continues on the other
side. Objects sit IN FRONT of the surrounding paint.
FACES STAY CLEAR: no wash touches or crosses a face or the hair on a head. Keep
clean white paper immediately around each face, about half a head's width.
Behind and between the shoulders the paint may come close.
Coverage: minimal and restrained — the halo touches only about A QUARTER of the
white area, and bare paper is by far the dominant surface around the figures.
Its weight sits near the bodies and thins to nothing toward the margins. The top
of the sheet, above the heads, stays almost entirely bare paper.
## PAPER AND FINISH
Pure bright white cold-press watercolor paper, its tooth visible only where
pigment sits. Premium, restrained, gallery wall-art: the emotional weight lives
in the faces, the energy in the paint that wraps them. Editorial illustration
quality, not cartoon, not photographic, not digital-airbrush.
## REMINDER
Vertical 3:4. The figures fill the sheet — about three quarters of its width and
most of its height, never small in the middle of an empty page. Generous white
paper above the heads, a faint warm halo of colour around them that fades
outward. Keep every object the subjects hold or stand behind.
